There shall be some who ask, "Who are you to tell us this?"

Fair. Here's my answer.

I am Yosef, founder of Kinet. We build software products for non-technical founders. Our most recent project is [uatmodel.com](https://uatmodel.com). It's an exam simulation platform for Addis Ababa University's undergraduate admission test, or UAT. It's sitting at 8,500+ active registrations right now.

I've spent years building infrastructure for edtech startups. I've also been quietly studying how this sector is evolving, in Ethiopia and far beyond it.

This is everything I've gathered, organized to make you look at the sector differently.

## The world already broke education open

For a very long time, education belonged to the government and its flagship institutions. If you wanted to learn something that mattered, you went to a school or a college. That was the only path.

Technology and globalization changed that completely.

When the internet went mainstream, universities started teaching online, on their own portals and through partner platforms.

You can take courses from Stanford, Harvard, and Princeton for free right now.

Platforms like Coursera and edX made distribution easy. Then Udemy cut the last gatekeeper loose: anyone with real-world experience could teach. No teaching accreditation. No affiliation with any school required.

Somewhere along the line, students stopped caring about the difference between a trained teacher and a person who simply knows the field.

They started learning from an entrepreneur in his garage. From a YouTuber in his studio.

When YouTube alone wasn't enough, digital products took over.

Successful creators on YouTube, Instagram, and TikTok began selling paid courses bundled with communities and weekly workshops. That demand gave birth to Skool, Kajabi, and Whop.

These platforms let a course creator set up shop the same way they set up a social media account.

So today the global market is split. Universities offer online degrees. Individuals, training centers, and colleges all sell courses and coaching in the same space.

Ethiopia missed most of this wave.

Plenty of Ethiopians have *taken* online courses. But very few have stepped up to *provide* them. The biggest reason is the payment infrastructure we still lack. I'll cover that in a separate piece. For now, let's look at what technology is actually doing inside Ethiopian education.

## Where Ethiopia actually stands

Start with the big institutions.

Addis Ababa University has a portal that lets students register for academics and exams from anywhere. They take exams online. They get results on their phones.

The university has also started offering online courses, including software development. One of my friends took that exact course from Dessie.

This has spread beyond universities. Students can now check their grade 6 and 8 regional exam results and their grade 12 national exam results online. Kelem Meda Technologies is the company building these internet and Telegram-based result platforms.

What's still rare is teachers recording lectures and putting them on major local or international platforms.

The institutions are starting anyway. Addis Ababa University introduced the UAT, and the online prep economy built around it is growing fast. Havan Academy, an independent organization, now runs full exam preparation online. Its platform includes recorded videos, prepared notes, and questions so students can prepare from home.

Alongside it sit Zegju, Entrance Tricks, Future X, and others.

Outside exam prep, Gebeya led the online course space before it evolved and was eventually replaced by the now-dominant ALX Africa. ALX has become the go-to platform for learning software development, fully online. It now carries prestige on par with top institutions.

Technology, the great equalizer. Really.

Now individuals are entering. YouTube personalities like Yonas Moh sell fully online courses in graphic design and social media marketing, running on their own digital infrastructure.

Here's the gap I keep coming back to.

Most traditional training centers in Ethiopia are limited by physical space and staff. That forces them to cap their intake per cohort.

The new startups don't have this problem. Everything runs online, so intake scales without walls.

That's the opportunity Ethiopian training centers haven't seized yet.

They have slept on a golden opening.

## The infrastructure is finally arriving here

Platforms that let individual creators upload courses and digital products are appearing in Ethiopia.

They're young. They sit at the very front of redefining education locally.

Ye-Buna lets you upload digital products and course communities without the headache of building the infrastructure yourself. Arifget is arriving with similar tools.

In Ethiopia this infrastructure is still in its early days. Everywhere else, it's already mature. It's a steady source of income for countless households when combined with organic social media marketing.

The classroom walls are gone everywhere else. They're starting to fall here too.

The founders, teachers, and creators who build in that gap first will own it.
